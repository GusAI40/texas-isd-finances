# What this project is, in plain English

Written for anyone — a parent, a taxpayer, a board member. No background
needed. Every figure here is checked against the data the site actually
serves; where we are unsure, we say so.

*(Reading level: about grade 5. The rest of the docs in this folder assume
you write software.)*

## The big idea

Texas school districts spend about **$109 billion a year**. That money comes
from you, but it is very hard to find out where it goes.

The state does publish the numbers. They just arrive as enormous spreadsheets
that almost nobody can read.

This project turns those files into a website anyone can use. You pick your
school district, and it shows what your district takes in, what it spends,
what it borrows, and what students got for the money.

Every number comes from the state. We did not make any of them up.

## What we found

Here is the part most people do not expect.

**Spending more money does not clearly help test scores.** When a district
raised its spending, results barely moved. The effect was so small that we
cannot even be sure it was real.

So what does help? The strongest thing we found was **paying teachers more**.
Raise teacher pay by $5,000 and scores go up by about **0.9 points**. That is
a small effect, but it is the clearest one in the data. Smaller classes helped
a little too. Keeping teachers from quitting may help, though we are less sure
about that one.

Now the honest part. All of these effects are small. The real difference
between a strong district and a weak one is something we cannot see in the
data at all, and about **three quarters of it stays unexplained**. It might be
leadership. It might be the community around the school. We do not know, and
the site says so.

We think that admission matters. A report that claimed to explain everything
would be easier to read and would be wrong.

## The other things the site shows

**Who pays.** Across Texas, about **54%** of school money is local property
tax, **37%** comes from the state, and **9%** from the federal government. If
your district owns a lot of valuable property, some of your tax money leaves
town and helps pay for schools elsewhere. The site shows how much.

**What borrowing bought.** When a district wants to borrow money, it has to
ask voters. That ballot is the only public record of what the money was for —
the state does not track it. We collected **4,588** of those elections going
back to **1958**. Then we asked whether passing one improved results. We found
no clear change either way.

**How poor students do.** Most reports show the gap between rich and poor
students. We think that is the wrong number, because a district can have a
small gap simply by doing badly for everyone. So we show how a district's
low-income students actually do, measured against low-income students across
the state.

**The Houston takeover.** In 2023 the state removed Houston's elected school
board and put in its own managers. We compared Houston with **13** districts
of similar size and poverty that were *not* taken over. Houston improved more.
But students in special education did worse. We report both, because leaving
out the second part would make the first part misleading.

## What we cannot tell you

- **Why** a district does well. We can show that it does. The cause is not in
  these numbers.
- Whether spending *more* would help *your* district. Averages across 1,200
  districts do not decide anything about one of them.
- Anything about individual students, teachers, or schools. This is
  district-level public data only.

## Where the numbers come from

The Texas Education Agency, the Texas Comptroller, and the US Census Bureau —
all public records. Budgets cover fiscal years 2009 to 2025. Borrowing
elections go back to 1958.

This site is independent. It is not a state product, and it does not rank,
recommend, or endorse any district.
