What we did

Texas keeps every number about its school districts — spending, test scores, debt, buildings — in ten different state files that don't talk to each other. We joined them into one website, built a personal report for every district, and emailed each district's leader a link to their own report. Free. No sign-up. No catch.

Almost every email arrived: 653 of 671. Only 13 bounced. That is a very healthy delivery rate for any email campaign, anywhere.

The part most reports hide

Robots faked half our numbers. We caught them.

Here's something most marketing reports won't tell you: school districts use security software that opens every email and clicks every link — automatically, to check for viruses. In the raw numbers, those robots look exactly like excited readers.

And they inflated everything. At one point our dashboard said 12 people were reading closely. We dug in. Four of those “readers” clicked at 3:47 in the morning, one second apart, from neighboring counties — that's one security machine walking down a list, not four humans. Most of the rest had the page open but never moved down it.

The true number was 2. So we rebuilt our measuring stick to only count things robots can't fake — like actually scrolling through a report and staying on each part long enough to read it. We would rather tell you a small true number than a big false one. That's the same rule the whole website is built on.

The honest funnel

Of the most recent 100 emails — the ones with careful measurement built in:

Is 16 out of 100 good? For a cold email to a busy public official during the first week of school — yes, genuinely good. It means the subject line works and the offer is interesting.

What a real reader looks like

One school leader read everything

One evening this week, a superintendent in a small Texas district opened our email, thought about it for 21 seconds, and clicked. Then they went down their district's whole report — the headline numbers, where each dollar goes, what the money is buying — spending about two minutes reading carefully.

No robot moves like that. That slow, deliberate scroll is a person deciding whether to trust you. Two people did that. Our job now is to turn two into twenty.

What we learned, and what happens now

The problem isn't getting them to the door — it's the doorway. Thirteen people arrived at the site with a real browser; nine of them never scrolled past the first screen. So before we email the next group, we make the first screen earn the second one.

348 district leaders are still on the list. The sending machine is rebuilt so it can never email the same district twice, honors every opt-out the moment it arrives, and keeps its records in a place that can't be lost. When we press go, it runs itself.

Zero replies is not a verdict yet. The reply counter literally hasn't been switched on. We'll say the real number when we can measure it — not before.
